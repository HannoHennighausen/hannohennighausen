Tissues spread like liquids

Active wetting transition in cell aggregates

Spreading cell aggregates behave like droplets: they can stay as a compact cluster or spread out completely, like water on clean glass. This liquid-drop analogy was established by a series of in vitro experiments, which showed that three-dimensional cell aggregates placed on a substrate spread much as droplets do (Douezan et al., 2011; Douezan et al., 2012; Douezan & Brochard-Wyart, 2012; Gonzalez-Rodriguez et al., 2012; Beaune et al., 2014; Yousafzai et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Pahlavan & Murrell, 2026). The same physics has recently been implicated in mouse embryo implantation (Cavanaugh et al., 2026). We show that this partial-to-total wetting transition in active tissues is set by active forces and by dynamic material properties such as friction.

arXiv:2608.28912

Active forces in a living droplet

Cells generate active forces, and both cell–cell and cell–substrate adhesions are needed to transmit them across the cluster. So, unlike in a passive droplet, active forces are present everywhere in the tissue — and they are largest at the edge. Our model has three of them:

  1. Active traction. To migrate, the cells in contact with the substrate push against it to crawl forward. These tractions follow each cell’s front–back polarity, which experiments show points outward and is strongest at the edge.
  2. Contractility. Every cell is contractile; acting along the polarity field, this produces an active contractile stress.
  3. Surface tension. The contractility and cell–cell adhesion of the cells not touching the substrate act as an effective surface tension at the contact line, pulling inward or outward depending on the geometry.

In a passive droplet these forces act only at the contact line. Here they are generated throughout the basal layer and drive active flows inside the tissue.

Spreading velocity sets the fate of the cluster

The central idea is simple: the spreading velocity decides what the cluster does. A positive value means it spreads, a negative one means it retracts, and zero means it sits at a steady state. Locating these zeros of the spreading velocity $V(L)$ maps out the active wetting transition. We distinguish stable steady states (filled circles ●) from unstable ones (open circles ○).

Dissipation also controls the transition

Here is the most striking difference from passive wetting. Because the active forces drive flows in the basal layer, the magnitude of those flows is set by dissipative parameters — the tissue’s viscosity and friction. Tuning these can therefore stabilize or destabilize the wetting state. In a passive droplet, friction and viscosity would only change how fast the droplet relaxes toward equilibrium — never whether the transition happens at all. Activity overturns that: can you find a regime where lowering the friction coefficient $\xi$ makes the partial-wetting state disappear?
